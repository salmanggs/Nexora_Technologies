Does a local business actually need a website?

We build websites for a living, so treat this with appropriate suspicion. It is still the honest answer.

A shop owner asked us this last month, and the honest answer was no. He sells vegetables from a stall people walk past every day. His customers are not searching Google, and a website would sit there doing nothing. We told him so, and he did not spend the money.

That is genuinely how it goes sometimes. Here is how to work out which side you are on.

You probably do not need one if…

  • Your customers are all walk-ins from the immediate area.
  • You sell one simple thing at a price everybody already knows.
  • Nobody has ever asked you for a link, a catalogue, or a price list.
  • Your Facebook page already handles every enquiry comfortably.

In these cases a good Google Business profile with photos, correct opening hours and your phone number costs nothing and does most of what a website would.

You probably do need one if…

  • People ask “do you have a website?” before deciding to trust you.
  • You sell to other businesses, where a link makes you look established.
  • You have more than a handful of products, prices or services to explain.
  • Customers come from outside your immediate town.
  • You run ads and currently send that traffic to a Facebook page.
  • You are competing against businesses that already have one.

The clearest signal: you find yourself typing the same explanation into WhatsApp three times a week. That message is a webpage waiting to exist.

What a website is actually for

It is not decoration. A working business site does three specific jobs: it shows up when somebody searches for what you sell, it answers the questions you get asked constantly, and it gives people a way to contact you at 11pm when you are asleep.

Anything beyond those three is optional. That is why we push back on animated intros and sliders nobody scrolls past — they cost money, slow the site down, and do none of the three jobs.

The parts people underestimate

Speed. Most visitors arrive on mobile data. A site that takes six seconds to load loses a large share of them before they see anything. This is the single most common problem with the sites people ask us to fix.

Content. The design is usually the easy part; the delay is nearly always waiting for photos and text about your own business. Start collecting those before you commission anything.

Ownership. Make sure the domain is registered in your name, not your developer’s. We have met several businesses held hostage by a domain somebody else controls, and it is a miserable position to be in.

If you are still unsure

Ask yourself what would change the week after it launched. If you can describe something concrete — “people could see my full price list”, “I could stop retyping the same message” — it is worth doing. If the honest answer is “it would look more professional” and nothing else, spend the money on stock or advertising first.

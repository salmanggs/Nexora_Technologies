Building Ahwal e Shahkot: a news app for one town

Most app advice assumes a big city and a fast phone. Building for Shahkot meant throwing most of that out.

Local news in a small town travels by forwarded message. Someone photographs a notice, sends it to a group, it gets forwarded to four more groups, and by the time it reaches you the date has been cropped off and nobody knows who posted it originally. Half the time it is already out of date. Occasionally it is simply not true.

That was the problem we set out to fix — not “disrupt local media”, just make it possible to see what is actually happening in your own town, from a source you can check.

The constraints came first

Before writing any code we wrote down what we could not assume. This list ended up shaping every technical decision that followed.

  • The average user is on a phone that is three to five years old, with limited storage.
  • The connection is 3G more often than 4G, and it drops.
  • Many users read Urdu comfortably and English less so.
  • A significant number have never installed an app from a store themselves.
  • Nobody is going to create an account to read the news.

That last one mattered more than anything. Every sign-up screen you put in front of someone loses you a chunk of your audience. For a news app serving one town, an account gains you nothing and costs you readers, so there is no login at all.

If a feature exists to help us rather than the reader, it does not go in. That rule removed about a third of the original feature list.

What we actually built

The app is deliberately small. A categorised feed of local news and announcements, each item with a headline, an image and a body. Push notifications for anything urgent. A simple admin panel where a publisher writes a post and it is live in thirty seconds. That is close to the whole product.

The interface is Urdu-first, with type set large enough to read without squinting. Images are compressed hard before upload, because a 4MB photo on a 3G connection is the same as no photo at all. The feed caches locally, so opening the app with no signal still shows you yesterday’s news instead of a spinner.

The admin panel got more attention than the app

This surprised us. The person publishing is not a technical user and is often writing on a phone, standing somewhere, in a hurry. If publishing is awkward, nothing gets published and the app dies quietly. So the admin side got the same care as the reader side: three fields, one button, and a preview of exactly what the reader will see.

Two things we got wrong

We over-categorised at launch. The first version had nine categories because we assumed people would browse by topic. They do not. They open the app, read what is new, and close it. We cut it to four and engagement went up, because the feed stopped feeling empty in each individual tab.

We sent too many notifications in month one. Every post triggered a push. It felt useful to us and irritating to everyone else, and uninstalls followed. Now only genuinely urgent items push, and the rest simply appear in the feed. Fewer notifications, more retained users — an obvious lesson in hindsight.

What it taught us about every project since

Ahwal e Shahkot is why we ask about the oldest phone in your customer base before we ask about your favourite colour. Real users on real connections are a much harder test than any spec document, and building for them first makes everything else easier.

It is also the reason Apna Shehar exists. The app works because it serves one town properly. The obvious next question was whether the same thing could be made repeatable for any town — which is what we are building now.
